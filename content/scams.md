# Debt-relief ads, settlement companies, and scams

## What the Forbes comparison is—and is not

The [Forbes Advisor debt-relief comparison](https://www.forbes.com/advisor/debt-relief/best-debt-relief-companies/) is a useful **market map**, but it is not a recommendation that any listed company fits this Maryland situation. It compares mostly for-profit debt-settlement firms, alongside a nonprofit counseling option, and the page includes commercial “learn more” links. It does not answer the decisive local questions: SSI/Medicaid impact, Maryland title and judgment-lien risk, actual home equity, bankruptcy consequences, or whether the payment plan is sustainable.

The article’s listed settlement fees are commonly **14%–25% of enrolled debt**. On $40,000, that is roughly **$5,600–$10,000** before considering savings-account or other charges. It also describes programs that have the consumer set aside money while the company seeks settlements; many settlement programs require the person to stop paying cards. That is a strategy choice with consequences—not simply “consolidation.”

For this fact pattern, a ranking should be a prompt to ask questions, not a reason to enroll. Compare it first against free/low-cost nonprofit credit counseling, direct hardship requests, and a Maryland consumer/bankruptcy consultation. The CFPB explicitly warns that many ads described as consolidation are actually debt-settlement offers. [CFPB: consolidation ads](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/ive-seen-a-lot-of-advertisements-for-companies-that-consolidate-credit-card-debt-are-these-legitimate-en-1859/).

## Know the difference

| Service | What it normally does | Key risk in this situation |
|---|---|---|
| Credit counseling / debt-management plan (DMP) | Reviews budget; may seek lower rates/one monthly payment; generally aims to repay principal. | Still fails if the essentials-first budget has no surplus; fees may apply. |
| Debt consolidation loan | Replaces debts with a new loan. | A home-equity or friend-secured loan can turn unsecured debt into foreclosure-capable debt. |
| Debt settlement | Attempts to persuade creditors to accept less than owed, usually after funds accumulate. | Often involves nonpayment, added interest/fees, credit damage, collection/lawsuit risk, possible tax on forgiven debt, and settlement-company fees. |
| Credit repair | Challenges inaccurate credit-report information. | Cannot lawfully erase accurate late payments or debt; many promises are deceptive. |
| Bankruptcy lawyer / legal-aid screening | Evaluates discharge, assets, liens, and defenses under actual law. | Must disclose all facts; a complex home/benefits case should not be filed pro se. |

## Why settlement can be especially risky here

The CFPB explains that settlement companies often charge substantial fees and commonly encourage stopping card payments. That can add late fees and penalty interest, worsen credit, increase collection pressure, and lead to suit before there is money to settle. A lawsuit/judgment may create a lien issue for a homeowner even where Social Security income is protected. [CFPB: debt-relief programs](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-relief-program-and-how-do-i-know-if-i-should-use-one-en-1457/).

For a person on disability income, the plan must also fit the real monthly budget. “Set aside 1.5% of enrolled debt each month” would be about **$600/month** for $40,000, before any other household emergency. If that money is not consistently available after housing, food, medical care, utilities, insurance, taxes, and a buffer, it is not a safe settlement plan merely because a company accepts the account.

## Scam and high-pressure warning signs

Walk away—or pause and independently verify—if a company, caller, text, influencer, or “advisor”:

- Charges a fee before it has settled a debt or entered the consumer into a debt-management plan.
- Guarantees a percentage reduction, says it can settle every debt, or promises “pennies on the dollar.”
- Claims a “new government program” will erase credit-card debt or that it can stop every lawsuit/collection call.
- Says to stop talking to creditors, a lawyer, the court, or benefits agencies.
- Contacts unexpectedly and asks for a Social Security number, bank login, debit-card payment, gift card, wire, crypto, or remote computer access.
- Pushes a same-day signature, a deed transfer, a home lien, or a payment before providing a written contract and time for independent review.
- Uses a name confusingly similar to a government agency, legal aid, or a creditor.

The FTC says only scammers demand upfront payment before settling debts or entering someone into a debt-management plan; guarantees and unexpected calls/texts asking for sensitive information are also strong scam signals. [FTC: avoid debt-relief scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/consumer-alerts/2026/03/looking-debt-relief-heres-how-avoid-scam). The CFPB provides a similar checklist. [CFPB: settlement warnings](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-debt-relief-program-and-how-do-i-know-if-i-should-use-one-en-1457/).

## A five-step vendor check

1. **Name the service.** Ask: “Is this debt management, settlement, consolidation lending, credit repair, or legal representation?” Put the answer in writing.
2. **Get all costs in dollars.** Ask for enrollment, monthly, escrow/savings-account, settlement, cancellation, and loan/closing fees—then calculate the maximum cost on $40,000.
3. **Ask what happens to each card.** Will payments stop? Will cards close? Can a creditor sue? Who handles a summons? What is the refund policy?
4. **Verify independently.** Search the CFPB complaint database; check Maryland licensing/consumer resources with the [Office of Financial Regulation](https://labor.maryland.gov/finance/consumers.shtml); and ask a nonprofit counselor or lawyer, not a salesperson.
5. **Check the house and benefits first.** Do not agree to a home lien, accept a lump sum, or move money if SSI/Medicaid, home equity, title, or bankruptcy has not been reviewed.

## If money or information was already sent

Contact the bank/card issuer using a number you independently locate, ask what reversal/dispute options are available, preserve screenshots/contracts/receipts, and do not pay a “recovery” company to get funds back. Report suspected fraud to [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) and consider the Maryland Office of Financial Regulation or an attorney. A follow-on “recovery” offer that asks for money or personal information is commonly another scam. [FTC: recovery scams](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/refund-and-recovery-scams).
