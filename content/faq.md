# Frequently asked questions

## “Can they take my Social Security disability check?”

For ordinary private credit-card debt, Social Security benefits have strong federal protection. Direct deposit also triggers limited automatic bank-account protection under federal rules. But do not equate “my check is protected” with “nothing can happen”: non-benefit funds can be frozen, exemption procedures may still be needed, and special debts such as tax, certain federal debts, child support, and alimony can operate differently. See [disability income and benefits](./benefits/) and [key terms: protected amount](./key-terms/#protected-amount).

## “Does disability mean I do not have to pay credit cards?”

No. Disability does not itself cancel a card balance. It may make a payment unaffordable and can be central to a hardship, settlement, or bankruptcy analysis. The person should not sacrifice food, housing, medicine, utilities, insurance, or taxes to keep an unsecured card current.

## “I just know I get ‘Social Security disability.’ How do I know whether it is SSDI or SSI?”

Read the award letter, review the SSA account, or call SSA. A person can receive both. This distinction is urgent before accepting money: [SSI](./key-terms/#supplemental-security-income-ssi) has strict income/resource rules; [SSDI](./key-terms/#social-security-disability-insurance-ssdi) does not use the same resource test.

## “Should I stop paying the cards?”

There is no universal answer. Temporarily preserving essentials can be rational, but missed payments can add fees, hurt credit, trigger collection, and increase lawsuit risk. Do not ignore a summons. A short legal and credit-counseling screening can identify whether hardship, a DMP, settlement, sale/downsize, or bankruptcy is safer.

## “Can a debt-settlement company really reduce what I owe?”

Some creditors may settle some debts, but no company can guarantee it. Settlement commonly means accumulating money while payments stop; balances can grow and a creditor can sue before settlement. Fees can be substantial. Read [ads, settlement companies, and scams](./scams/) before sharing information or enrolling.

## “Is debt consolidation the same as debt settlement?”

No. [Debt consolidation](./key-terms/#debt-consolidation) replaces or organizes debt, often with a new loan or a debt-management plan. [Debt settlement](./key-terms/#debt-settlement) attempts to pay less than the full balance, often after missed payments. Ads use these phrases loosely, so ask for the service type in writing.

## “Will a debt-management plan save my house?”

A [debt-management plan](./key-terms/#debt-management-plan-dmp) usually does not put a lien on the home. It can help if its monthly payment actually fits after essentials. It is not a solution if the plan requires money the person does not have, or if material home equity/judgment risk requires bankruptcy/legal planning instead.

## “Can a credit-card creditor put a lien on my home?”

Usually a creditor must first sue and obtain a judgment. A recorded judgment can affect real property and future sale/refinance. Existing mortgages, true net equity, the deed, marital title, and exemptions all matter. See [Maryland collection, judgments, and the home](./creditors/).

## “Should I borrow against the house from a friend?”

Only after independent legal, tax, benefits, title, and bankruptcy review. It may replace high-rate cards with a clearer debt, but it can also turn potentially dischargeable unsecured debt into [secured debt](./key-terms/#secured-debt) that threatens foreclosure. First see if a limited gift for professional advice or an unsecured option meets the need.

## “If a friend gives me money, will I lose SSI or Medicaid?”

Possibly. SSI gifts and retained cash can affect income/resource eligibility; a valid loan has different treatment but unspent proceeds may still count as a resource next month. Medicaid and other programs can have their own rules. Get benefits guidance **before** receiving or moving money, changing title, or closing a sale.

## “Could bankruptcy take my house?”

Possibly, if there is enough nonexempt equity after mortgages, other liens, selling costs, title share, and the applicable bankruptcy exemption. It is never safe to assume based only on a Zillow-style value. A home with $25,000 gross equity and a home with $400,000 gross equity call for very different analysis. See [resolution paths and bankruptcy](./options/).

## “What if I already received a court notice or my bank account is frozen?”

Calendar the deadline, keep the papers, preserve benefit-deposit records, and seek prompt Court Help or legal assistance. Do not transfer money/property, sign a new lien, or rely on a salesperson’s promise to “stop the lawsuit.” The [resource list](./resources/) has starting points.

## “What is the lowest-risk first request I can make to friends?”

Ask for time and a limited contribution toward independent legal, benefits, appraisal, tax, or counseling costs—not an immediate $40,000 loan. Give a genuine no-pressure option to decline. See [help from friends without pressure](./friends/).
